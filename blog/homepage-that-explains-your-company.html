The homepage test: can a stranger say what you do?

The first screen of a company website has one job. A person who has never heard of you should be able to say what you do, who it is for, and what they should do next. Logos, a paragraph about values, and three identical cards usually fail that job.

Run the test before you argue about colours

Open the homepage on a phone. Hand it to someone outside the company. Ask them to say, in one sentence, what the company sells. If they talk about the photograph, or about “quality and trust”, the sentence is missing. Design will not rescue a missing sentence.

Write three lines, then stop

  1. What you do, in the words a customer would use.
  2. Who it is for: a city, a trade, or a kind of company.
  3. The next step: call, WhatsApp, or a page that continues the explanation.

Everything else can move down. A long story about the founder belongs further on, or on the about page. The first screen is not the place for it.

Proof has to be real

A number you invented is not proof. A client you can name, a project a visitor can open, or a review count that matches the public listing — those are proof. If you have none yet, do not borrow any. Say what you do and how to reach you. An enquiry path beats a fake statistic.

A homepage is not a landing page

A homepage introduces the company. A landing page argues for one offer. If you only have one offer this month, a landing page may be the smaller build. If you have several services, the homepage should point to them, not pretend they are one sentence.

When the sentence is clear, the rest of what a business website needs is easier to decide. If you want that sentence written against your actual work, start with the website.
